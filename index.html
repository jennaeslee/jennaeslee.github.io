Wildfire risk & evacuation priority, California

A composite index that combines wildfire hazard and population density to rank California’s 58 counties by disaster-management priority.

  • Weighted overlay
  • Zonal statistics

Mean composite risk index (scale 1–5). The top four counties, in blue, are all in the Sierra Nevada foothills.

Read the write-up
Question
Where do high wildfire hazard and many people overlap, so that evacuation planning and resources should go first?
Method
Projected all layers to NAD 1983 Contiguous USA Albers and clipped them to California. Census block-group population density (log-transformed) and USDA Wildfire Hazard Potential were reclassified to 1–5, then combined with a weighted overlay (60% hazard, 40% density). Zonal statistics gave a mean index per county.
Finding
Risk is highest in the Sierra Nevada foothills and around the San Francisco and Sacramento areas, and lowest in the southeastern desert. CAL FIRE historical perimeters largely overlap the high-risk zones. Calaveras, El Dorado, Amador and Nevada counties rank highest. Many foothill areas are served by only one or two main roads.
Limits
Only primary and secondary roads were used, so forest service and unpaved roads are missing. Population density stands in for exposure; the number of people inside high-risk zones was not counted.
Next
Count exposed population, add a fuller road network, include age and income, and test future climate scenarios.

SO₂ pollution trends across the U.S.

How SO₂ emissions in the contiguous U.S. change from 1981 to 2100 in a climate model, and how they relate to population.

  • GFDL-ESM4
  • SSP5-8.5
  • Python
  • Linear regression
Read the write-up
Question
How do SO₂ levels change over time across the U.S., and how do they relate to where people live?
Method
Used GFDL-ESM4 total SO₂ emission rate (historical and SSP5-8.5 runs, 1° × 1.25° grid) and compared annual means, differences between periods, and emissions by sector. State population and SO₂ levels were compared with per-capita values and linear regression.
Finding
The model shows SO₂ emissions falling by the end of the century, with the largest decreases over the eastern and Midwestern U.S. The eastern U.S. loses more than 20 tons per square mile per year, and the national average falls from 0.67 (historical) to 0.34 (future) in model units. Total emissions drop from about 3.1 to about 1.5 Mt per year between 2020 and 2100, and industry is the largest sector. Less populated states such as Alaska, Wyoming and Vermont have higher SO₂ per person, and the regression showed no meaningful link between population and SO₂ level.
Limits
The model cannot include future policy and its resolution is coarse. Population alone leaves out industry, technology and regulation, which likely explain more of the pattern.
Next
Add economic, technology and policy factors (for example the Clean Air Act and state rules) in a causal model.
